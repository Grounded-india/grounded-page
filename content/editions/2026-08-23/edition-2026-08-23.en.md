On August 20, 2026, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) reported ongoing trends in the money market, reflecting the continued operational scale of India’s digital payment infrastructure. This context underscores the enduring relevance of a regulatory framework established nearly seven years earlier, when the RBI mandated the National Electronic Funds Transfer (NEFT) system to operate on a 24x7 basis starting December 16, 2019. The directive, formalized in circular DPSS (CO) RPPD No.1097/04.03.01/2019-20 issued on December 6, 2019, transformed NEFT into a round-the-clock service, aligning India’s payment ecosystem with global standards for real-time transactions (rbi).

The December 2019 circular built upon an earlier notification from August 30, 2019 (DPSS (CO) RPPD No.510/04.03.01/2019-20), which first announced the plan to extend NEFT availability beyond traditional banking hours. Implementation details outlined in the December circular specified that settlements would occur in 48 half-hourly batches daily, with the first batch initiating after 00:30 hours and the last concluding at 00:00 hours. The system was designed to function without interruption, including weekends and public holidays, relying on Straight Through Processing (STP) modes for transactions initiated outside regular banking hours. Banks were instructed to maintain adequate liquidity in their current accounts with the RBI to ensure seamless batch settlements and to disseminate information about the extended timings to customers (rbi).

The operational guidelines emphasized continuity in procedural discipline, requiring banks to credit beneficiary accounts or return transactions within two hours of each batch settlement. Positive confirmation messages (N10) were mandated for all NEFT credits, and existing procedural rules remained applicable. The directive, issued under Section 10(2) read with Section 18 of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, underscored the central bank’s authority to regulate payment systems in the public interest. By August 2026, the NEFT system—processing millions of daily transactions—remained a cornerstone of India’s financial architecture, supported by the infrastructure and compliance mechanisms outlined in the 2019 circular (rbi).

The RBI’s regulatory framework for payment systems has evolved through such circulars, which serve as binding instructions for member banks. The 2019 directives followed a period of rapid digitization in Indian banking, accelerated by policies like the launch of the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) in 2016. While the August 20, 2026, market trends report did not specifically reference the NEFT system’s performance, the data on liquidity conditions and interbank transactions indirectly reflected the stability of the payment infrastructure enabled by the 24x7 operational mandate. The RBI’s Notifications section, accessible on its official website, continues to archive these circulars, ensuring transparency and ongoing compliance (rbi).

As of the latest reporting period, no revisions or updates to the 2019 NEFT guidelines have been announced, suggesting the framework remains effective. However, the RBI’s periodic reviews of payment system regulations—and the dynamic nature of India’s digital economy—leave room for future adjustments, though none have been indicated to date.

### Report

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) yesterday published draft directions outlining prudential norms for dividend declarations by Regional Rural Banks (RRBs), exercising powers under Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949. The proposed norms, titled *Reserve Bank of India (Regional Rural Banks – Prudential Norms on Declaration of Dividend) Directions, 2026*, will apply to all RRBs across India and take effect from the financial year 2026-27 (rbi).

The draft, issued under reference number DOR.ACC.REC.No./21.02.067/2025-26, includes a chapter on eligibility criteria requiring RRBs to maintain compliance with regulatory capital requirements both at the end of the previous financial year and during the year in which dividends are proposed. Boards of directors must also evaluate supervisory findings from NABARD on asset classification divergences, audit reports, capital adequacy projections, and long-term growth plans before approving dividends (rbi). Definitions within the document specify terms such as “Adjusted Profit After Tax (PAT),” which excludes net non-performing assets, and clarify that “dividend” encompasses both final and interim payouts (rbi).

The RBI stated it issued the draft after being “satisfied that it is necessary and expedient in the public interest,” though it did not elaborate on specific triggers for the move. The draft is part of the central bank’s broader regulatory framework for RRBs, which play a critical role in rural credit distribution but have faced challenges in maintaining capital adequacy amid rising non-performing assets in recent years. The norms aim to ensure dividend distributions do not compromise banks’ financial stability or ability to meet regulatory requirements (rbi).

Public comments on the draft are invited, though the RBI has not specified a deadline for submissions. The document, available in the “Home - Press Releases - Content” section of the RBI website, includes sections on commencement, applicability, and definitions, with Section 3 explicitly limiting its scope to RRBs (rbi).

While the draft does not introduce immediate changes, its implementation from FY 2026-27 could tighten dividend policies for RRBs, potentially affecting shareholder payouts and governance practices. The RBI has not indicated whether further revisions will follow stakeholder feedback, leaving uncertainty about the final form of the regulations.

Kaziranga’s nine corridors, critical for the movement of endangered one-horned rhinos, elephants, and other wildlife, remain under threat from encroachments and infrastructure projects.
