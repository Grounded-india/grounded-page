### Report

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney stood firm Tuesday as his government imposed retaliatory tariffs on approximately $20 billion in U.S. goods, declaring, “You’re at war when you get attacked. We got attacked.” The measures, targeting American steel, dairy products, appliances, and farm equipment, mark a sharp escalation in tensions between the longtime allies, though analysts suggest a full-blown trade war remains avoidable (ap_india).

The tariffs follow the collapse of negotiations last week and a U.S. move over the weekend to levy 50% duties on select Canadian imports. President Donald Trump responded to Ottawa’s retaliation by warning Canadian leaders to “fall in line” or face consequences “far WORSE,” including potential new taxes on vehicles and auto parts. Ontario Premier Doug Ford amplified the rhetoric, threatening to cut off electricity and critical mineral exports to the U.S., while Trump jokingly proposed renaming Lake Ontario “Lake America” in a jab at his counterpart (ap_india).

Despite the heated language, experts note room for de-escalation. The U.S. tariffs currently affect only about 5% of Canada’s exports to the U.S., with Oxford Economics estimating a minimal drag on Canadian growth—from 1.6% to 1.4% next year. Canadian retaliation, set to take effect September 8, provides a near-term window for diplomacy. “If there is political will, there is an off ramp,” said former U.S. trade negotiator Wendy Cutler (ap_india).

Historical precedent supports cautious optimism. The two nations have repeatedly clashed over issues like Canada’s dairy protections and softwood lumber subsidies, only to reach compromises. “It’s almost like it’s not real until somebody walks away from the table,” said Christopher Sands of Johns Hopkins University’s Center for U.S.-Canada

## 2. Trump Administration Pauses Visa Appointments Worldwide

*Yesterday, the US State Department announced a temporary halt to visa appointments globally to accommodate staff training initiatives at embassies and consulates.*

### Report

Applicants for U.S. visas worldwide received emails yesterday informing them of cancelled interviews as the Trump administration instituted a global pause on visa appointments, citing a training initiative for consular officers (reuters_india). The U.S. State Department announced the move as part of a broader effort to align visa evaluation processes with stricter public charge assessments, though it did not specify the duration of the pause or details of the training curriculum.

The pause applies to all visa appointment scheduling at U.S. embassies and consulates globally, with affected applicants notified of rescheduling via email (reuters_india). The State Department described the training as necessary to ensure officers can “screen out applicants deemed likely to become dependent on U.S. public benefits” and evaluate applications “comprehensively and consistently” (google_news_india). This marks the second time the Trump administration has linked visa processing delays to public charge enforcement, following a similar pause in immigrant visa applications earlier this year that was later blocked by a federal judge (google_news_india).

The training initiative coincides with proposed fee hikes for H-1B visas, which allow U.S. employers to hire foreign workers in specialized roles. The Department of Homeland Security has floated an additional $103,265 fee for cap-subject H-1B petitions, which would disproportionately impact Indian professionals, who account for a majority of such visas (reuters_india). The Indian IT industry body NASSCOM has urged the U.S. government to reconsider, arguing that the fee increase would burden American companies reliant on global talent pools (reuters_india).

The visa appointment pause is part of President Trump’s renewed immigration crackdown during his second term, which has included efforts to revoke B1 and B2 visas for up to 200,000 foreigners who have applied for asylum (reuters_india). While the administration frames these measures as protecting domestic resources, critics note that public charge determinations have historically been applied narrowly, focusing on direct cash assistance or institutionalization, rather than broader social benefits (google_news_india).

Legal challenges to the Trump administration’s immigration policies have had mixed success. A federal court in January blocked a rule that would have denied immigrant visas to applicants from 75 countries deemed high-risk for public charge dependency, ruling it “arbitrary and capricious” (google_news_india). The current pause on appointments, however, does not target specific nationalities but instead delays all visa processing to implement the training.

As of yesterday, the State Department has not provided a timeline for completing the training or resuming normal visa operations. Applicants in India, a major source country for both immigrant and non-immigrant U.S. visas, face renewed uncertainty amid overlapping policy changes, including the proposed H-1B fee hike, which remains under public comment review (reuters_india). The outcome of legal disputes over the training mandate and fee proposals will likely shape the future of U.S. immigration processing in the coming months.

### Report

Students gathered in protest at the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Delhi campus late on Tuesday night, following the suicide of a student earlier in the day, according to a report by *The Indian Express*. The demonstration, which began around midnight, drew dozens of students to the campus’s central areas, where they raised slogans demanding improved mental health support and accountability from the institution’s administration (indian_express).

The deceased student, whose identity has not been disclosed, was found in their hostel room on Tuesday morning. While the exact circumstances of the death remain under investigation, the incident has reignited concerns about the academic and social pressures faced by students at premier technical institutes. Protesters alleged that the institution had failed to address longstanding gaps in counseling services and academic stress management, despite repeated appeals from the student body (indian_express).

By Wednesday morning, the campus remained tense, with security personnel deployed near administrative buildings. Students speaking to *The Indian Express* described a sense of “collective grief and anger,” emphasizing that the suicide was not an isolated incident but part of a pattern of neglect. “We’ve lost too many peers to silence,” one protester said, though they declined to be named (indian_express).

The event underscores a persistent challenge for India’s top educational institutions, where high academic expectations and competitive environments often collide with inadequate mental health infrastructure. Over the past five years, multiple IITs and other elite colleges have reported student suicides linked to stress, financial burdens, or discrimination. In 2023, a parliamentary panel urged reforms to strengthen counseling systems and reduce stigma around seeking help, but implementation has been uneven across campuses (indian_express).

IIT Delhi’s administration has not publicly commented on the protest or the suicide as of Wednesday afternoon. Students have called for an independent inquiry into the incident and the institution’s mental health policies. It remains unclear whether the protest will escalate or if dialogue between students and authorities will begin in the coming days (indian_express).
