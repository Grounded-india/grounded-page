SAARC, founded in 1985 with seven members—Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka—and later expanded to include Afghanistan in 2007, was conceived to foster economic growth, social progress, and cultural exchange in a region marked by geopolitical tensions. These dynamics led to the exclusion of bilateral issues from SAARC deliberations, a rule that while intended to depoliticize proceedings, also limited the organisation’s ability to address pressing cross-border challenges.

Despite hosting 18 summits since 1985, SAARC’s operational record remains mixed. It has established four specialised bodies and five regional centres focused on areas like disaster management and public health, but its impact has been constrained by persistent political rivalries, particularly between India and Pakistan. The cancellation of the 2016 summit—after India and Afghanistan boycotted the event in Islamabad following a terrorist attack in Uri—marked a symbolic low point, effectively freezing institutional activity. The Maldives’ recent mediation offer acknowledges this stalemate but does not resolve the core issue: member states’ reluctance to subordinate national interests to regional solidarity. As noted by *The Hindu*, even if a summit is reconvened, SAARC’s viability will depend on smaller nations assuming greater responsibility for building mutual trust, a task complicated by divergent economic priorities and security concerns.

The grouping’s challenges extend beyond India-Pakistan tensions. Domestic politics in member states often overshadow regional initiatives, with leaders prioritising immediate electoral or strategic gains over long-term cooperation. For instance, Nepal’s internal debates over federalism and Sri Lanka’s economic crisis have diverted attention from SAARC-led projects. Meanwhile, India’s growing bilateral engagements—such as the Indo-Pacific partnership and bilateral infrastructure investments—have reduced the perceived utility of a multilateral forum like SAARC. Analysts argue that without a shift toward issue-based cooperation on transnational issues like climate change, trade, or pandemic response, SAARC risks remaining a ceremonial entity.

As the Maldives’ call for revival gains traction, questions linger over whether member states can reconcile their competing visions for the organisation. While the absence of a clear roadmap for resuming summits or reforming SAARC’s consensus-driven decision-making process leaves its future uncertain, the renewed focus on regional collaboration reflects a recognition that South Asia’s shared challenges—from rising sea levels to economic inequality—demand collective action. For now, the grouping remains at a crossroads, its potential unrealized and its relevance contingent on political will that has historically been in short supply.
